# M2 outcome: M1b's "the stream contributes 0.11%" was a saturation artifact — the stream alone causes the full harm

Pre-registration `M2_STREAMRATE_PREREG_2026-08-25.md` (`983f764`). mos181, victim
`pointer_chase` 170 MB on cpu8, F = 16 workers on 32–47, n=6, order rotated,
24/24 records.

## The numbers

| arm | n | victim cyc/load | CoV | harm |
|---|--:|--:|--:|--:|
| V alone | 6 | 78.079 | 0.02% | 1.0000 |
| V+F_hr05 (hit 0.5, stream on) | 6 | 209.441 | 0.22% | 2.6824 |
| V+F_hr10 (hit 1.0, stream on) | 6 | 216.622 | 0.58% | 2.7744 |
| **V+F_hr10_ns** (hit 1.0, **stream off**) | 6 | **77.552** | 0.02% | **0.9933** |

Registered reading, `contribution(hr) = 1 − nostream/stream`:

| | contribution |
|---|--:|
| at hit rate 0.5 (M1b) | **−0.11%** |
| **at hit rate 1.0 (M2)** | **+64.20%** |

Registered threshold ≥10% → **the stream matters.** Lever verified as required:
F's stream rate rose **5.217 → 8.482 GB/s** (1.63×) and its cost per access fell
89.47 → 55.20 cyc.

## Why the two disagree, and why M1b was wrong

**The hit rate silently controls F's table footprint.** In an open-addressing
table a *hit* lands at or near its hash position, but a *miss* walks the linear
probe chain until it finds an empty slot. So at hit rate 0.5, half of F's probes
scatter across the **whole 256 MiB table**. At hit rate 1.0 they touch only the
~65,536 entries belonging to the keys in play — about **1 MiB** (confirmed: the
no-stream arm reports `local_n = 65536`, `fact_bytes = 1048576`).

That means M1b's "no stream" control was **not** a low-footprint arm. It still
had a 256 MiB scattered table hammering the shared cache. And the victim
**saturates**: it is LLC-resident at 78 cyc/load or DRAM-bound at ~209, with
nothing in between. Both the stream *and* the scattered table independently push
it over that edge.

So M1b compared two arms that were both saturated, by different routes, and
concluded the difference between them — 0.11% — meant the stream was irrelevant.
It meant no such thing. **M1b's headline is withdrawn.**

M2 removes the second route (hit rate 1.0 collapses the table footprint) and the
stream's effect becomes visible immediately: **with the stream, 2.774×; without
it, 0.993×.** The stream *alone* is sufficient to cause the entire harm.

Symmetrically, M1b's own data already showed the scattered table alone is also
sufficient (2.680× with no stream at all). The two causes are **independent and
redundant**, which is exactly why removing either one alone appeared to do
nothing.

## What this restores, and the one thing it does not show

**Restored:** the three-party stakeholder has a cause the mechanism can name. A
fused tenant's stream, on its own, degrades a latency-critical neighbour by
**2.77×**. That is precisely the harm H2 was designed to remove, and the earlier
finding that the stream costs *F itself* nothing means removing its allocation
should be close to free for F.

**Not shown, and the distinction is the whole ballgame:** `--no-stream` removes
the **bytes**. H2 would still read every byte and merely decline to *allocate*
them in the shared cache. So M2 measures **an upper bound on what H2 could
achieve**, not H2's benefit. A mechanism that stops the bytes is not on offer;
one that stops the allocation is.

**The decisive experiment is therefore the non-allocating-stream arm** — F reading
all 256 MiB but not retaining it, via flush-behind (`clflushopt` at a distance) or
`MOVNTDQA` on write-combining pages. If the neighbour's harm drops toward 1.0
while F still reads every byte, H2 works and the mechanism has its configuration.
If the harm stays at 2.7×, then it is the *bytes in flight* rather than the
*cache residency* that hurts, and no admission-control mechanism helps.

This is the arm the review panel proposed as "flush-behind-on-F". It is now the
single most important open experiment in the project, and `stream_wb_flushbehind`
already exists in the tree.

## Correction record

This is the sixth time in three days that reading one layer deeper reversed a
conclusion, and the second where a *control* was the thing at fault rather than
the measurement. M1's control never ran; M1b's control was active but not
controlling — it removed one of two redundant causes. The lesson is narrower than
"read the artifact": **a control must be shown to have removed the thing it
names, and in a saturating metric that means checking the footprint, not just the
flag.**
